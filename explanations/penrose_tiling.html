Aperiodic Tilings

Penrose Tiling

What is a Penrose Tiling?

A Penrose tiling is an example of an aperiodic tiling, discovered by Roger Penrose in the 1970s. While it is constructed using a small set of prototiles (usually two shapes), the resulting pattern never repeats exactly, even if extended to infinity. This is known as "non-periodic" behavior.

The Golden Ratio

Penrose tilings are deeply connected to the Golden Ratio (φ ≈ 1.618). In the P3 version (rhombus tiling), the ratio of the number of thick rhombuses to thin rhombuses approaches φ as the pattern grows. Every single tile edge is in a golden ratio relationship with some other part of the geometry.

Forbidden Symmetry

Before Penrose, it was thought that tilings could only have 2-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold, or 6-fold rotational symmetry. Penrose tilings famously exhibit 5-fold (decagonal) symmetry, which was previously considered "impossible" for a structure that fills the entire plane without gaps.

Quasicrystals

For a long time, Penrose tilings were considered mathematical curiosities. However, in 1982, Dan Shechtman discovered materials called quasicrystals that have the same aperiodic structure as Penrose tilings. This discovery was so revolutionary that it earned him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011.
