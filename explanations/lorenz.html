The Lorenz Attractor

Chaos, Predictability, and the Butterfly Effect

What is it?

The Lorenz Attractor is a set of chaotic solutions to the Lorenz equations, a system of three ordinary differential equations first studied by Edward Lorenz in 1963. It was originally derived from a simplified model of atmospheric convection.

When plotted in 3D space, the attractor resembles a butterfly or a mask. It is perhaps the most famous example of a strange attractor—a complex fractal structure that a chaotic system eventually settles into.

The Equations

The system is defined by:

dx/dt = σ(y - x)
dy/dt = x(ρ - z) - y
dz/dt = xy - βz

Classic parameters for the "Butterfly" are: σ = 10, ρ = 28, and β = 8/3.

The Butterfly Effect

Lorenz discovered that even tiny changes in the starting conditions (initial values) would lead to completely different outcomes. This "sensitive dependence on initial conditions" became known as the Butterfly Effect.

The idea is that a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas weeks later. In mathematics, this means that long-term weather forecasting is practically impossible, as we can never measure the current state of the world with perfect precision.
